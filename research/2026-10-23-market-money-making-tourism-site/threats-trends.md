## Trends, Threats & Downside

This axis researches, skeptically, the forces that could make a new solo/bootstrapped travel website fail. The short version: the two years from 2023 to 2025 were arguably the worst stretch in the history of independent web publishing, and travel/affiliate content sat squarely in the blast radius of both Google's Helpful Content Update and the arrival of AI answers. The evidence below is weighted toward recent data and toward surfacing downside. Where the strongest numbers come from the affected parties themselves or from SEO vendors with an interest in the story, that is flagged.

### (a) AI Overviews / AI search and the hit to travel-content traffic

The clearest causal evidence comes from the Pew Research Center, whose July 2025 study of 68,879 real Google searches by 900 US adults in March 2025 found that when an AI Overview appeared, users clicked a traditional search result on only 8% of visits, versus 15% when no summary appeared — roughly halving the click-through rate — and clicked a link *inside* the AI summary on only about 1% of visits; sessions also ended more often on AI-summary pages (26% vs 16%). At the time of the study, 18% of searches generated an AI summary. [The Register — Pew AI Overviews study](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/google_ai_overviews_suppress_search/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: High, tier: Established, carrying Primary Pew data); [Search Engine Roundtable — Pew](https://www.seroundtable.com/pew-google-ai-overviews-39808.html) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: High, tier: Established). This is correlation in browsing behavior, not proof that summaries alone cause the drop, and the panel is self-selected rather than a random sample — a caveat Pew itself notes.

At the publisher level, the single most authoritative recent number is Chartbeat data published in the Reuters Institute's *Journalism and Technology Trends and Predictions 2026* report: global Google search referrals to publishers fell about 33% in the year to November 2025, with the US decline steeper at 38%, and Google Discover referrals down 21% globally. Surveyed media leaders (280 across 51 countries) expect traffic to fall 43% on average over the next three years, with about a fifth expecting losses above 75%. Notably, that same report found AI-assistant referrals are still negligible — ChatGPT at 0.02% and Perplexity at 0.002% of total referral traffic — meaning the traffic lost to AI answers is *not* being meaningfully recaptured through AI citations. [Press Gazette (Chartbeat/Reuters Institute data)](https://pressgazette.co.uk/media-audience-and-business-data/google-traffic-down-2025-trends-report-2026/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: High, tier: Established). The report flagged lifestyle/utility publishers (weather, TV guides, horoscopes) as more exposed but gave no travel-specific figure.

Supporting CTR studies broadly agree on direction while disagreeing on magnitude: Seer Interactive reported organic CTR down about 61% on queries with AI Overviews, while Ahrefs measured roughly a 34.5% drop in position-one CTR — a two-fold spread that reflects different methodologies and query sets. [thedigitalbloom roundup](https://thedigitalbloom.com/learn/2025-organic-traffic-crisis-analysis-report/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Low, tier: Low — a vendor aggregator; the underlying Seer/Ahrefs studies are Established but I did not open them directly). On spread, Semrush data cited across multiple vendors shows AI Overviews appearing on about 6.49% of queries in January 2025, 13.14% by March, and roughly 30% of US desktop queries by September 2025 — a fast ramp. Similarweb reportedly found 83% of AI-Overview queries end without a click. These spread figures are consistent across several secondary carriers (confidence: Medium, tier: Established underlying, Low carriers).

No source I found isolates travel-blog organic traffic specifically with a clean percentage; the travel-specific evidence is the HCU case material in (b). Treat "travel organic traffic is down X%" as inferred from the publisher-wide 33–38% Chartbeat figures plus the HCU case studies, not directly measured.

### (b) Helpful Content Update and core updates 2023–2025: documented damage to independent travel/affiliate sites

This is the best-documented threat on the whole axis, and it is severe. The template case is HouseFresh (an air-purifier review site, not travel, but the canonical "HCU devastation" story). By its own first-party account it fell from about 4,000 daily Google visitors to roughly 200 — a ~95% collapse (the site headlines it as a 91% loss of search traffic) — with the damage landing after the September 2023 HCU and deepening on March 9, 2024 with Google's March 2024 core update; most residual traffic was people searching the brand by name. [HouseFresh — "How Google decimated HouseFresh"](https://housefresh.com/how-google-decimated-housefresh/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: High for the site's own numbers, tier: Primary — but it is the aggrieved party). Its earlier essay argued Google's core/HCU updates reward brand recognition and established domains (Dotdash Meredith, BuzzFeed, Popular Science) running "best of" lists not based on first-hand testing, over independent testers. [HouseFresh — "David vs Digital Goliaths"](https://housefresh.com/david-vs-digital-goliaths/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary/opinion); this argument was reported (not independently verified) by [Nieman Lab, Feb 2024](https://www.niemanlab.org/2024/02/google-promotes-sketchy-product-reviews-from-big-publishers-at-the-expense-of-small-indie-sites-a-small-indie-site-argues/) (accessed 2026-10-23 via search summary — page returned 403 on direct fetch, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). Retro Dodo told similar stories (reported 85–90% organic losses and laying off all staff but the founder and one editor, per CNET as relayed by PPC Land) (confidence: Medium, tier: Established carrier).

For travel specifically, a Travelpayouts post (vendor blog) cites a "Digital Loft" analysis naming Culture Trip down 70%, PlanetWare down 90%, and The Crazy Tourist down almost 100% after the HCU, arguing that high domain authority gave no protection and that affiliate links/ads were *not* the clean trigger (ad-free sites were hit; affiliate-heavy sites sometimes survived); it says listicle-heavy travel sites and single-destination niche blogs were hit hardest. [Travelpayouts — HCU and travel blogs](https://www.travelpayouts.com/blog/hcu-and-other-google-updates/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Low, tier: Low — vendor, secondary attribution). A separate widely-repeated claim that of 671 analyzed travel publishers, 213 (32%) lost more than 90% of organic traffic appears only in secondary SEO write-ups with no stated methodology (confidence: Low, tier: Low). An aggregate "400+ sites" study claims only 22% of HCU-hit sites recovered even 20% of lost traffic, and that affiliate review sites averaged roughly −58%. [thestacc](https://thestacc.com/blog/helpful-content-update-recovery/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Low, tier: Low — single vendor study).

The mechanism is Primary and important: with the March 2024 core update Google folded the Helpful Content *system* into its core ranking systems, so it stopped being a standalone, periodically-run classifier and became an always-on, site-wide signal — meaning a tranche of "unhelpful" pages can drag down an entire domain. Google framed the change as aiming to reduce low-quality/unoriginal content by roughly 40–45% and warned of unusual ranking volatility during the ~45-day rollout. [Search Engine Land — March 2024 core update](https://searchengineland.com/google-released-massive-search-quality-improvements-with-march-2024-core-update-and-multiple-spam-updates-438144) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: High, tier: Established, quoting Google/Danny Sullivan directly). Winners of the churn, per Sistrix analyses relayed across sources, were large platforms and UGC/authority sites — Reddit, Spotify, Wikipedia — i.e. exactly the destinations a new independent site cannot be.

### (c) Zero-click search

Zero-click was already structural before AI and is worsening. SparkToro/Datos put US zero-click at 58.5% in 2024 (EU 59.7%), meaning only about 360 of every 1,000 US searches sent a click to the open web; SparkToro's 2026 update (Similarweb panel, Jan–Apr 2026) reports 68.01% of US Google searches ended with no click, up from 60.45% in 2024. [SparkToro figures via multiple aggregators](https://www.elitecontentmarketer.com/zero-click-search-statistics/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established underlying / Low carriers — I did not open SparkToro's primary post). A genuine conflict exists on definition: a narrower desktop-only Datos/SparkToro measure put US zero-click at only 27.2% in March 2025, because it counts only session-ends-without-forwarding rather than all no-click searches — so headline "68%" and "27%" describe different things and should not be mixed. For news-type searches Similarweb reported zero-click rising from 56% to ~69% by May 2025. Net: even before AI, roughly four to six of every ten searches never reach a website, and the trend line is up.

### (d) Display-ad revenue pressure and RPM trends

Here the skeptical finding cuts *against* the doom narrative on price, though not on volume. Broad programmatic display pricing has not collapsed: Guideline/eMarketer data showed digital display eCPMs up 2.2% QoQ and 3.4% YoY in Q2 2024 even as overall digital eCPMs fell 6% YoY. [eMarketer — Guideline eCPM H2 2024](https://www.emarketer.com/content/guideline-ecpm-pricing-data-h2-2024) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). One supply-side data point points the other way — PubMatic reported display revenue down about 8% YoY in a quarter, blamed on a single large DSP buyer's weak holiday spend (confidence: Low, tier: Primary but single-company/single-quarter). Creator-network RPMs appear roughly flat-to-slightly-up, not crashing: one blogger's Mediavine monthly RPM averaged ~$33 (Oct 2023–Oct 2024) and ~$35 the following year (~6% gain), with a Raptive-vs-Mediavine test showing ~$32 vs ~$24 average RPM and January the weakest month. [thisweekinblogging](https://thisweekinblogging.com/mediavine-vs-raptive/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Low, tier: Low — individual dashboards). The real downside is arithmetic, not price: ad revenue is pageviews × RPM, so a stable ~$25–35 session RPM applied to traffic that has fallen 33–95% still means revenue collapse. Display ads monetize volume, and volume is the thing under attack. (Note also that Mediavine/Raptive require ~50k and historically higher sessions/month to join, so a *new* site cannot access premium RPMs at all for a long time.)

### (e) Platform/channel risk: Google, affiliates, social

A travel site is exposed on three dependent channels at once. (1) **Google**: HouseFresh noted search was still ~64% of its traffic even post-recovery, which Search Engine Journal flagged as a continuing single-point-of-failure risk — the dependency does not go away even after you win. (2) **Affiliate programs**: Amazon Associates was restructured in 2026 with commission cuts of up to 50% for some publishers, removal of milestone bonuses, gutted SKU/ASIN-level reporting, and an April 14, 2026 Operating Agreement change limiting commission to the same ASIN or direct variants (ending "halo" earnings on other items in the cart); third-party tables put the top standard rate near 10%, down from a historical 20% ceiling. Amazon told Adweek this affects "a tiny fraction of associates." [Adweek — Amazon Associates cuts](https://www.adweek.com/media/amazon-associates-affiliate-rate-cuts-publishers/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established); [January Digital](https://januarydigital.com/amazon-affiliate-commission-cuts/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Low, tier: Low). Travel affiliates (Booking.com, hotel/OTA, tour aggregators) were not covered in these sources, but the lesson generalizes: affiliate terms are set unilaterally and can be cut overnight. (3) **Social**: Facebook referrals to publishers were down roughly 43% versus May 2023 (and had fallen ~50% YoY for news sites during 2024, with The Independent's Facebook share of social referrals falling from 19% in 2021 to 6% in 2024), though they rebounded ~9% YoY by November 2025 after Facebook re-surfaced news. Pinterest — often cited as the travel blogger's social channel — now sends marginal traffic (reportedly under 0.5% of publisher referrals in one dataset). [Press Gazette — publisher traffic sources](https://pressgazette.co.uk/media-audience-and-business-data/media_metrics/publisher-traffic-sources-2019-2025/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium-High, tier: Established); [Digiday — Facebook referral decline](https://digiday.com/media/publishers-reckon-with-declining-facebook-referral-traffic-as-the-platform-pulls-away-from-news/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). Every one of these channels is controlled by a third party whose incentives are not aligned with a small publisher's survival.

### (f) Which travel content is MOST vs LEAST exposed to AI-answer substitution

No travel-specific trigger study surfaced, so this is inference from general intent data plus the mechanics of AI answers. Informational queries trigger AI Overviews far more than any other intent — estimates range from ~88–91% of informational queries in early 2025 (some datasets as high as 99.2% of informational keywords), falling to roughly 57% by October 2025 as Google expanded AIOs into commercial queries — while transactional queries trigger them least (~14–16%), commercial ~22%, and navigational ~10–12%; longer and question-format queries trigger more often. [Semrush — AI SEO statistics](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-seo-statistics/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established); [Search Engine Land — AI Overviews guide](https://searchengineland.com/guide/google-ai-overviews) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). Applied to travel: the staples of SEO travel blogging — "best time to visit [place]", "things to do in [city]", "how to get from X to Y", "is [place] safe", "[country] packing list" — are exactly the fact-seeking, question-format informational queries an AI answers completely in the SERP, so they are the *most* substitutable. Least exposed are transactional/booking-intent queries ("book [hotel]", "cheap flights to X on [dates]"), interactive tools (calculators, live price/availability, itinerary builders, maps), deeply personal/experiential narratives, and very fresh or hyper-local first-hand information an LLM's training data lacks. This is analysis, not a measured travel-sector finding (confidence: Low–Medium).

### (g) Seasonality and structural risks of travel as a niche

Travel content earnings are inherently seasonal and lumpy. Practitioner reports (all anecdotal, single-site, Low tier) describe a "September slump" as summer planning ends and school resumes, traffic tapering through autumn and recovering after Christmas, and a December pattern of lower traffic but strong income (high ad rates) followed by a weak January. [Two Wandering Souls income report](https://twowanderingsoles.com/blog/travel-blog-income-report-q3-2019) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Low, tier: Low). Broad multi-destination coverage partially hedges the swing; single-season or single-destination sites swing hardest. Beyond seasonality, structural risks specific to travel include: long content lifecycles that go stale (prices, visa rules, opening hours) and demand constant upkeep a solo builder may not sustain; a crowded, mature niche with entrenched incumbents and heavy AI-generated spam; macro shock sensitivity (a pandemic, recession, conflict, or airfare spike can zero out travel demand — COVID-19 being the obvious precedent); and the fact that travel's highest-value, most-searched informational queries are precisely the ones most exposed to AI substitution per (f). The combination — slow to monetize, high maintenance, cyclically and macro-exposed, and sitting on the most AI-substitutable query set — makes travel a harder bootstrapped bet in 2026 than it was in, say, 2018.

### (h) Contrarian evidence: travel content is still viable, and what survivors do differently

The downside is real but not total, and there is credible contrarian signal. The strongest proof point is that HouseFresh fully recovered: on October 11, 2025 it reported Google traffic back to (and past) pre-HCU peak visibility, roughly two years and one month after the collapse — described by one recovery study as the only widely-verified full return to pre-HCU levels. [PPC Land — HouseFresh recovery](https://ppc.land/housefresh-achieves-notable-traffic-recovery-after-google-algorithm-impacts-2/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). Crucially, during the crisis it diversified, building a ~30,000-subscriber YouTube channel. The pattern across survivor commentary is consistent: the moat is first-hand, demonstrably-experienced content (genuine testing, original photography, on-the-ground reporting) that AI cannot synthesize and that aligns with Google's E-E-A-T emphasis — an argument made by Amsive's HCU analysis and echoed by travel-writing commentators that "writing with human connection, soul and personalized storytelling" is what survives. [Amsive — HCU analysis](https://www.amsive.com/insights/seo/how-googles-latest-helpful-content-system-update-is-impacting-serps/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established).

On format, the industry data favors video and owned audiences over SEO text. Skift reports more than half of US travelers in its 2025 tracker have used AI tools for trip planning and its State of Travel 2026 puts global familiarity at 62%, with social platforms becoming primary discovery tools — i.e. demand is moving to TikTok/YouTube/Reddit and AI assistants, away from blog SEO. [Skift — How AI changed travel planning](https://skift.com/2025/02/03/how-has-ai-changed-travel-planning/) (accessed 2026-10-23, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). Vendor-cited figures (treat as Low–Medium) claim 71% of travelers say video influenced their destination choice vs 24% for static images (Expedia 2025), and that 62% made a specific trip decision after viewing social content (Phocuswright 2024). Email remains the most defensible channel: one destination-marketing guide reports 41% of marketers call email their most effective channel, far ahead of social and paid search. The through-line for survivors is de-risking off Google: brand/direct traffic, an email list, a YouTube/short-form video presence, community, and interactive tools or booking-intent pages that AI cannot fully answer. A pure programmatic-SEO-plus-display-ads-plus-Amazon-affiliate travel blog — the classic 2018 playbook — is the single most-exposed model to everything in sections (a) through (f).

### Most-exposed vs most-defensible — synthesis

Most exposed: a new, brand-less site relying on Google organic for informational "best time to visit / things to do / how to get to" listicles, monetized by display ads (volume-dependent) and Amazon-style affiliate links (terms cut unilaterally), with no email list, no video, and no first-hand experience signals. This model is simultaneously hit by AI Overviews (halved CTR on exactly these queries), the HCU-in-core site-wide classifier (which favors Reddit/Wikipedia/big brands over new domains), rising zero-click (now ~60–68% US), seasonality, and affiliate/social channel risk — with essentially no AI-referral traffic flowing back in (ChatGPT 0.02% of referrals). Most defensible: a branded site built on genuine first-hand experience and original media, earning direct/brand and email traffic, extending into YouTube/short-form video, offering interactive tools or transactional/booking-intent pages (least AI-substitutable), and treating Google as one channel among several rather than the foundation. HouseFresh's two-year near-death-and-recovery is the cautionary and the hopeful case at once: survivable, but only with diversification and patience most bootstrappers do not have.

> Conflicts noted: (1) AI-Overview CTR impact ranges widely by source — Pew's ~8% vs 15% (roughly −47% relative), Ahrefs ~−34.5% position-one, Seer ~−61% — reflecting different methods; the Pew figure is the most rigorous and is used as the baseline. (2) Zero-click headline numbers conflict by definition — SparkToro's "all no-click" measure (~60–68% US) vs the narrower Datos desktop measure (~27%); these are not comparable and must not be averaged. (3) Display-ad evidence conflicts with the doom narrative: programmatic display pricing/RPMs are roughly flat-to-up (Guideline/eMarketer, blogger dashboards), while one supply-side quarter (PubMatic −8%) and the overall −6% YoY digital eCPM point down — the real revenue threat is traffic volume, not unit price. (4) Named travel-site loss figures (Culture Trip −70%, PlanetWare −90%, etc.) rest on a single vendor's secondary attribution and should be treated as illustrative, not measured.
